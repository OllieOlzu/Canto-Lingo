# Cantonese Grammar Course --- Specific Words, Terms, and Particles

This file covers the specific Cantonese words and grammatical terms that
can be confusing for English speakers. A Cantonese word can have several
translations, and one English word can translate into several Cantonese
words.

## Rule 1. `m4` and `mei6` are not interchangeable

Both can be translated using "not" or "haven't", but they work
differently.

### 唔 --- `m4`

Usually negates a verb, preference, or state.

-   `ngo5 m4 zung1ji3` --- I don't like it.
-   `ngo5 m4 heoi3` --- I'm not going.
-   `m4 hai6` --- isn't / is not.

### 未 --- `mei6`

Often means "not yet", or asks whether something has happened yet.

-   `ngo5 mei6 sik6 faan6` --- I haven't eaten yet.
-   `nei5 sik6 zo2 faan6 mei6?` --- Have you eaten yet?
-   `keoi5 mei6 dou3` --- They haven't arrived yet.

**Rule:** `m4` generally negates something; `mei6` often tells you that
something hasn't happened by the relevant time.

Compare:

-   `ngo5 m4 sik6` --- I don't eat / I can't eat, depending on context.
-   `ngo5 mei6 sik6` --- I haven't eaten yet.

## Rule 2. 想 `soeng2` = want to, would like to, or think

`soeng2` expresses a desire, intention, or thought. The meaning depends
on the sentence.

-   `ngo5 soeng2 sik6 faan6` --- I want to eat.
-   `ngo5 soeng2 heoi3 Hoeng1gong2` --- I want to go to Hong Kong.
-   `ngo5 soeng2 keoi5 hai6 hou2 jan4` --- I think they're a good
    person.

**Rule:** `soeng2` is often softer than `jiu3` when expressing a desire.

## Rule 3. 要 `jiu3` = want, need, or must

This word is more direct than `soeng2` in many everyday contexts.

-   `ngo5 jiu3 jat1 bui1 seoi2` --- I want / need a glass of water.
-   `ngo5 jiu3 zou6 gung1fo3` --- I need to do homework.
-   `nei5 jiu3 faai3 di1` --- You need to be faster.

Compare:

  ------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Cantonese                Meaning                 Typical feeling
  ------------------------ ----------------------- -----------------------
  `ngo5 soeng2 heoi3`      I want to go.           Desire or intention

  `ngo5 jiu3 heoi3`        I need / want to go.    More direct; need or
                                                   intention

  `ngo5 m4 soeng2 heoi3`   I don't want to go.     Lack of desire

  `ngo5 m4 jiu3`           I don't want it.        Refusing something

  `m4 sai2`                No need to.             Not necessary
  ------------------------------------------------------------------------

The exact meaning of `jiu3` depends on the situation. It can express a
want, a need, or an instruction.

## Rule 4. 使 `sai2` in `唔使` means "need to" --- but the whole phrase means "don't need to"

This is a particularly useful expression:

**唔使 --- `m4 sai2` --- don't need to / no need to.**

-   `nei5 m4 sai2 heoi3` --- You don't need to go.
-   `m4 sai2 haang1 zeon6` --- No need to hurry.
-   `m4 sai2 m4 goi1` --- No need to, thanks. (Often used in a polite
    exchange.)

Compare these three:

-   `m4 jiu3` --- don't want it.
-   `m4 soeng2` --- don't want to / don't feel like it.
-   `m4 sai2` --- don't need to.

These are **not interchangeable**. If someone offers you food and you
say `m4 jiu3`, you're declining it. If you say `m4 sai2`, you're saying
you don't need it.

## Rule 5. 有 `jau5` and 冇 `mou5` = have and don't have

Cantonese commonly uses these to express possession and existence.

-   `ngo5 jau5 jat1 go3 gau2` --- I have a dog.
-   `ngo5 mou5 cin2` --- I don't have money.
-   `dou6 dou6 jau5 jan4` --- There are people everywhere.

But `jau5` also appears in expressions about experiences and states. For
example, `jau5 sik6 gwo3` means "have eaten before" in the appropriate
context.

**Rule:** think of `jau5` as "have / there is" and `mou5` as "don't have
/ there isn't", rather than treating them only as possession verbs.

## Rule 6. 得 `dak1` has several common meanings

This is one of those words where context really matters.

### A. Can / be able to

-   `ngo5 dak1` --- I can / I am able to. (What you can do must be clear
    from context.)
-   `nei5 dak1 m4 dak1?` --- Can you do it or not?

### B. A result or degree after a verb

The particle `得` can follow a verb and introduce a description of how
well or how an action is performed.

-   `keoi5 paau2 dak1 hou2 faai3` --- They run very fast.
-   `nei5 zou6 dak1 hou2 hou2` --- You do it very well.

Here, `dak1` isn't simply the standalone verb "can". It links an action
to its result or description.

### C. "Only" or "just" in some expressions

In certain constructions, `dak1` contributes a meaning like "only",
"just", or "as little as". You should learn those expressions
individually rather than applying one translation everywhere.

## Rule 7. 俾 `bei2` can mean "give" or "let / allow"

-   `ngo5 bei2 zo2 bun2 syu1 keoi5` --- I gave them the book.
-   `nei5 bei2 ngo5 tai2 haa5` --- Let me have a look.
-   `m4 hou2 bei2 keoi5 heoi3` --- Don't let them go.

The sentence structure helps you determine whether `bei2` means "give",
"let", or "allow".

## Rule 8. 返 `faan1` and 翻 `faan1` can have different functions

You will often hear `faan1` in spoken Cantonese. It can express
returning to a place, doing something again, or restoring an earlier
state, depending on the expression.

-   `faan1 uk1 kei2` --- go home / return home.
-   `zou6 faan1` --- do again / redo, depending on context.
-   `seon3 faan1` --- restore / recover in some contexts.

Don't assume every `faan1` means "return". Learn the whole phrase.

## Rule 9. 啲 `di1` is a very common plural, quantity, and degree word

English uses "some", "a few", "these", and plural `-s`. Cantonese often
uses `di1` for a range of related meanings.

-   `hai1 di1 syu1` --- these books / some books, depending on context.
-   `bei2 ngo5 di1 seoi2` --- give me some water.
-   `faai3 di1` --- faster / a bit faster.
-   `m4 hou2 gam3 daai6 seng1` --- don't speak so loudly.

The important point is that `di1` doesn't have one fixed English
translation. It often marks an indefinite quantity, a plural group, or a
degree.

## Rule 10. Comparisons use 過 `gwo3` and 冇咁 `mou5 gam3`

To say one thing is more than another, Cantonese often uses:

**A + adjective + 過 + B**

-   `ngo5 gou1 gwo3 nei5` --- I'm taller than you.
-   `ni1 go3 gwai3 gwo3 go2 go3` --- This one is more expensive than
    that one.

To say something is not as much as another thing, you can use:

-   `ngo5 mou5 gam3 gou1` --- I'm not that tall.
-   `ni1 go3 mou5 gam3 gwai3` --- This one isn't that expensive.

For a simple "more than" comparison, `gwo3` follows the adjective. For
"not so ...", `mou5 gam3` is a useful everyday pattern.

## Rule 11. `dim2 gaai2` means "why", and `dim2 joeng2` means "how"

Question words are useful to learn as complete expressions.

-   `dim2 gaai2?` --- Why?
-   `dim2 joeng2?` --- How?
-   `bin1 dou6?` --- Where?
-   `gei2 do1?` --- How many / how much?
-   `mat1 je5?` --- What?
-   `bin1 go3?` --- Who?

Unlike English, Cantonese commonly keeps a question word in the position
where the answer would go.

-   `nei5 heoi3 bin1 dou6?` --- Where are you going?
-   `nei5 zung1ji3 mat1 je5?` --- What do you like?
-   `keoi5 hai6 bin1 go3?` --- Who is it?

You generally don't move the question word to the beginning just because
English does.

## Rule 12. `ge3` connects descriptions to nouns

The particle 嘅 `ge3` is extremely common. One important function is to
mark possession or connect a description to a noun.

-   `ngo5 ge3 syu1` --- my book.
-   `nei5 ge3 gau2` --- your dog.
-   `hou2 leng3 ge3 faan6` --- a beautiful meal.
-   `ngo5 zung1ji3 ge3 jan4` --- the person I like.

There are more complex uses, but this is a good starting point.

**Rule:** `ge3` often connects information before it to the noun that
follows. It is not always translated into a separate English word.

## Rule 13. `dou1` means "also", "all", or "even" depending on context

This is another word worth learning early.

-   `ngo5 dou1 zung1ji3` --- I also like it.
-   `keoi5 dei6 dou1 lai4 zo2` --- They all came.
-   `gam2 dou1 dak1?` --- Is even that okay? (depending on context)

It often appears after the subject and before the verb or adjective:

**Subject + `dou1` + predicate**

For example, `ngo5 dou1 sik6` means "I eat too" or "I also eat",
depending on the conversation.

## Rule 14. Sentence-final particle 啊 / 呀 `aa3` can soften a sentence or add emphasis

`aa3` is one of the most common particles. It can make a sentence sound
more conversational, friendly, emphatic, or expressive.

-   `hou2 aa3` --- Okay! / That's good!
-   `nei5 hou2 aa3` --- You're fine / you're good, depending on context.
-   `hai6 aa3` --- Yes, that's right.

The exact effect depends on the speaker's tone and the sentence. Don't
translate every `aa3` as a separate English word.

## Rule 15. 呀 `aa3` often makes speech more conversational

The written particle 呀 is also commonly represented as `aa3` in
Jyutping, although its pronunciation can vary with context.

-   `nei5 heoi3 bin1 dou6 aa3?` --- Where are you going?
-   `gam2 aa3` --- Like that? / Is that so? (depending on context)

You may see 呀 used after questions, statements, and expressions of
surprise. In many cases it makes the sentence flow more naturally.

## Rule 16. 啦 `laa1` can signal a change, a decision, or an urging tone

Think of `laa1` as a particle that often signals "now", "that's it",
"come on", or a transition --- depending on context.

-   `zou2 laa1!` --- Let's go! / Time to go!
-   `sik6 faan6 laa1` --- Time to eat! / Eat now!
-   `hou2 laa1` --- Okay, that's enough / all right.

**Rule:** `laa1` often presents something as happening now, being
decided, or being urged. It doesn't always literally mean "now".

## Rule 17. 喎 `wo3` often marks new information or a reminder

`wo3` is especially useful for understanding conversational Cantonese.
It can suggest that something is noteworthy, newly realised, or being
pointed out.

-   `nei5 hai6 hok6saang1 wo3` --- You're a student, you know! / You're
    actually a student!
-   `ngo5 ting1 jat6 m4 dak1 haan4 wo3` --- I'm not free tomorrow, just
    so you know.

The English translation changes according to context and intonation.
Often, there is no single English word that captures it.

## Rule 18. 咩 `me1` can turn a statement into a surprised or challenging question

This is a very useful particle for real conversations.

-   `hai6 me1?` --- Really? / Is that so?
-   `nei5 zung1ji3 keoi5 me1?` --- You like them? (surprised or
    checking)
-   `m4 hai6 me1?` --- Isn't that so? / Isn't it?

The particle often conveys surprise, disbelief, or a request for
confirmation. A plain yes/no question and a `me1` question can feel
quite different.

## Rule 19. 囉 `lo1` often signals an obvious conclusion or acceptance

`lo1` can convey that something follows naturally from the situation, or
that the speaker is accepting the result.

-   `gam2 jau5 laa1` --- Then that's it / so that's how it is, depending
    on context.
-   `hai6 lo1` --- That's right / exactly, depending on context.

It is difficult to give `lo1` one exact English translation. The
important thing is to notice the speaker's attitude: it can sound
matter-of-fact, resigned, or like "well, obviously".

## Rule 20. 嘛 `maa3` can mean "after all", "you know", or "that's because..."

This particle can present something as obvious, expected, or
explanatory.

-   `hai6 gam2 maa3` --- That's how it is, after all / that's right,
    isn't it.
-   `keoi5 hai6 hok6saang1 maa3` --- They're a student, after all.

Its effect depends on the sentence and context, so don't memorise one
English equivalent.

## Rule 21. 呢 `ne1` can mark a follow-up question or a continuing topic

This particle is common in questions, especially when asking about
another person or situation.

-   `nei5呢?` --- What about you?
-   `keoi5呢?` --- What about them?
-   `gam2 nei5 soeng2 dim2 aa3?` --- So what do you think?

The last example uses `aa3` rather than `ne1`, but it illustrates how
conversational particles help questions sound natural.

## Rule 22. 啫 `ze1` often means "just", "only", or "that's all"

-   `ngo5 hai6 gong2 haa5 ze1` --- I'm just saying.
-   `gam2 ze1` --- Just that / that's all, depending on context.

This particle often downplays the importance of something or limits what
the speaker means.

## Rule 23. 嗻 `ze1` and 啫 `ze1` may look or sound similar in romanisation

Jyutping doesn't always tell you the written character from sound alone.
Some particles share a pronunciation or have closely related
pronunciations, and their spelling depends on usage and convention.

When learning particles from captions, focus first on the sound and the
conversational meaning. Learn the Chinese character when you can
identify it reliably.

## Rule 24. `zo2`, `gwo3`, and `gan2` mark aspects of an event

### 咗 --- `zo2`

Often marks a completed event or a change of state.

-   `ngo5 sik6 zo2 faan6` --- I have eaten / I ate a meal.

### 過 --- `gwo3`

Often marks past experience: having done something before.

-   `ngo5 heoi3 gwo3 Hoeng1gong2` --- I've been to Hong Kong before.

### 緊 --- `gan2`

Often marks an action in progress.

-   `ngo5 sik6 gan2 faan6` --- I'm eating a meal.

**Rule:** these markers don't correspond perfectly to English tense. For
example, `zo2` doesn't simply mean "past tense"; the context determines
how the event relates to the present.

## Rule 25. `faan1` can express returning, repeating, or restoring

You will often hear `faan1` in spoken Cantonese. It can express
returning to a place, doing something again, or restoring an earlier
state, depending on the expression.

-   `faan1 uk1 kei2` --- go home / return home.
-   `zou6 faan1` --- do again / redo, depending on context.
-   `seon3 faan1` --- restore / recover in some contexts.

Don't assume every `faan1` means "return". Learn the whole phrase.

## Rule 26. `hou2` doesn't always mean "good"

`hou2` can mean "good", but it can also work like "very" or "quite"
before an adjective.

-   `ngo5 hou2 hoi1sam1` --- I'm happy.
-   `gam1 jat6 hou2 jit6` --- It's hot today.
-   `ni1 go3 hou2 gwai3` --- This is expensive.

In `hou2 jit6`, it intensifies the adjective "hot"; it doesn't mean
"good hot".

## Rule 27. Sentence-final particles change conversational meaning

Consider:

-   `hai6` --- it is / yes.
-   `hai6 aa3` --- yes, that's right.
-   `hai6 me1?` --- really? / is that so?
-   `hai6 lo1` --- exactly / that's how it is, depending on context.

The core statement is similar, but the particle changes the
conversational effect. Learn the particles in complete sentences and
situations, not as one-to-one translations.
