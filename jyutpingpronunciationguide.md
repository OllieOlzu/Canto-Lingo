# The Complete Guide to Cantonese Jyutping Pronunciation

Jyutping (粵拼) is a system for writing Cantonese pronunciation using the Latin alphabet and tone numbers. Once you understand its rules, you can read the pronunciation of almost any Cantonese word, even if you've never seen it before!

This guide focuses on how to turn Jyutping into the sounds you actually say, including initials, vowels, final consonants, tones, and changes that happen in natural speech.

One important distinction: Jyutping is a spelling system, not English phonics. For example, `j` in Jyutping does not sound like the English `j` in *jam*.

## 1. How a Jyutping Syllable Works

A typical Jyutping syllable has three parts:

- **Initial:** the beginning consonant
- **Final:** the vowel, sometimes with an ending consonant
- **Tone:** the pitch pattern, represented by a number

For example, `goi2` (改) means “change.”

Examples:

- `sik6` (食) — to eat
- `nei5` (你) — you
- `hou2` (好) — good
- `m4` (唔) — not
- `hou2 sik6` (好食) — delicious / good to eat

The letters tell you which sounds to make, and the number tells you which tone to use.

## 2. All 19 Jyutping Initials (Beginning Consonants)

Jyutping has 19 standard initials, plus syllables that begin directly with a vowel.

| Jyutping | How to pronounce it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `b` | Like English **p** in *spy*, with little puff of air | `baa1` — dad |
| `p` | Like English **p** in *pie*, with a strong puff of air | `paa3` — fear |
| `m` | Like English **m** | `maa1` — mum |
| `f` | Like English **f** | `faa1` — flower |
| `d` | Like English **t** in *stop*, with little puff | `daa2` — hit |
| `t` | Like English **t** in *top*, with a strong puff | `taa1` — he |
| `n` | Like English **n** | `nei5` — you |
| `l` | Like English **l** | `laa1` — sentence particle |
| `g` | Like English **k** in *sky*, with little puff | `gaa1` — home |
| `k` | Like English **k** in *key*, with a strong puff | `kaa1` — card |
| `ng` | Like the ending of *sing*, moved to the beginning | `ngaa4` — tooth |
| `h` | Like English **h** | `haa1` — laugh |
| `gw` | A `g` sound immediately followed by `w` | `gwaa1` — melon |
| `kw` | A `k` sound with a puff, immediately followed by `w` | `kwaa1` — exaggerate |
| `w` | Like English **w** | `waa1` — frog |
| `z` | Like **ts** in *cats*, without a strong puff | `zaa1` — dregs |
| `c` | Like **ts** in *cats*, with a strong puff | `caa1` — fork |
| `s` | Like English **s** | `saa1` — sand |
| `j` | Like English **y** in *yes* | `jaa5` — also/too |

Some example syllables have multiple meanings depending on context; the examples illustrate the sounds rather than define every possible meaning.

### The Most Important Consonant Rules

**Rule 1: `b`, `d`, and `g` aren't pronounced like English B, D, and G.**

These are unaspirated consonants. The air is released with less force than for `p`, `t`, and `k`.

Try saying these pairs:

- `b` versus `p`
- `d` versus `t`
- `g` versus `k`

Put your hand in front of your mouth. You should feel a stronger puff of air with `p`, `t`, and `k`.

**Rule 2: `z`, `c`, and `s` are different sounds.**

- `z`: unaspirated *ts*
- `c`: aspirated *ts*
- `s`: ordinary *s*

Don't pronounce `z` as the English letter Z in *zoo* if you want the standard Jyutping sound.

**Rule 3: `j` always represents the English Y sound.**

For example:

- `jat1` — one
- `ji6` — two
- `jyu5` — fish
- `jyut6` — month

Never pronounce the `j` in these words like the J in *jump*.

**Rule 4: `ng` is a single consonant.**

It represents the nasal sound you hear at the end of *sing*, but you must be able to start a syllable with it.

- `ng5` — five

Practise starting with the nasal sound without adding an extra vowel such as “uh”.

**Rule 5: `gw` and `kw` are each one initial.**

Pronounce the consonant and the `w` together, not as two separate syllables. For example, `gwai3` (expensive) has one syllable.

**Rule 6: Some speakers pronounce `n` as `l`.**

You may hear `nei5` (you) pronounced more like `lei5`. This is a common variation in Hong Kong Cantonese, not a change in the official spelling.

## 3. All the Basic Vowel Sounds

The vowels are where English speakers often struggle, because Jyutping uses combinations of letters to represent sounds that English spells differently.

The English comparisons below are approximations. Listen to native-speaker audio to learn the exact vowel quality.

| Jyutping | Approximate sound | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `aa` | Long open *ah*, as in *father* | `baa1` |
| `a` | Shorter, central vowel, roughly the vowel in *but* | `bat1` |
| `e` | Like the vowel in *bed* | `se1` |
| `i` | Like *ee* in *see* | `si1` |
| `o` | Like *o* in *go*, but without the English glide | `go1` |
| `u` | Like *oo* in *food* | `fu3` |
| `oe` | Rounded lips, with a vowel between *er* and *aw* | `hoe1` |
| `eo` | A short central, rounded vowel, roughly like some British pronunciations of *fur* | `ceot1` |
| `yu` | Say *ee* while rounding your lips as if saying *oo* | `syu1` |

### Rules for Reading Vowels

**Rule 7: `aa` and `a` are different vowels.**

- `saan1` — mountain
- `san1` — new

The extra `a` in `aa` means the vowel is longer and has a different quality. Don't treat it as a spelling decoration.

**Rule 8: `e` and `i` are different.**

The vowel in `se1` is closer to the vowel in *bed*, while `si1` has the *ee* sound.

**Rule 9: `o` and `u` are different.**

- `go1` — older brother
- `gu1` — a syllable with the *oo*-like vowel

Don't automatically give both vowels the English *oo* sound.

**Rule 10: `oe`, `eo`, and `yu` need special practice.**

These are not interchangeable.

- `hoe1` — shoe
- `heoi3` — to go
- `syu1` — book

For `yu`, the tongue position is close to `i`, but your lips are rounded. This is similar to the vowel in French *tu* or German *ü*.

For `oe` and `eo`, pay attention to the exact vowel quality and length. English doesn't have perfect equivalents.

## 4. All the Vowel Combinations (Diphthongs)

A diphthong is a vowel sound that moves from one vowel position to another within the same syllable.

| Jyutping | How to practise it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `aai` | `aa` moving towards `i` | `laai5` — come |
| `aau` | `aa` moving towards `u` | `maau1` — cat |
| `ai` | `a` moving towards `i` | `tai2` — look |
| `au` | `a` moving towards `u` | `gau2` — dog |
| `ei` | `e` moving towards `i` | `fei1` — fly |
| `eu` | `e` moving towards `u` | `deu6` — throw away |
| `oi` | `o` moving towards `i` | `hoi1` — open |
| `ou` | `o` moving towards `u` | `hou2` — good |
| `ui` | `u` moving towards `i` | `bui1` — cup |
| `iu` | `i` moving towards `u` | `giu3` — call |
| `eoi` | `eo` moving towards `i` | `seoi2` — water |

**Rule 11: The final letters belong to the same syllable.**

For example, `hou2` is one syllable, not “ho-oo”. Move smoothly between the vowel positions.

**Rule 12: The starting vowel determines how you begin.**

Compare:

- `ai` and `aai`
- `au` and `aau`
- `oi` and `ou`

The spelling gives you information about the vowel's quality and how it moves. Do not simply pronounce every vowel letter independently.

## 5. All the Final Consonants: `-m`, `-n`, `-ng`, `-p`, `-t`, `-k`

This is one of the most important parts of Jyutping. A final consonant changes how a syllable ends, and you must pronounce it correctly to distinguish many Cantonese words.

### Nasal Endings

| Ending | How to pronounce it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `-m` | Close your lips to finish the sound | `sam1` — heart |
| `-n` | Put your tongue against the area behind your upper teeth | `saan1` — mountain |
| `-ng` | Finish with the nasal sound in *sing* | `seng1` — to rise |

**Rule 13: Don't add an extra vowel after a final consonant.**

For `sik6`, don't say “si-kuh”. Finish with the `k` sound.

**Rule 14: `-n` and `-ng` are different.**

- `-n`: the tongue touches the front of the mouth.
- `-ng`: the back of the tongue rises towards the soft palate.

Compare `an` and `ang`, or `in` and `ing`. Practise them slowly until you can hear and produce the difference.

### Stop Endings

| Ending | What to do | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `-p` | Close your lips to stop the sound | `sap6` — ten |
| `-t` | Stop the sound with the tongue behind the upper teeth | `sat6` — to lose |
| `-k` | Stop the sound with the back of the tongue | `sik6` — to eat |

**Rule 15: Stop endings are short and unreleased.**

In standard Cantonese, final `-p`, `-t`, and `-k` are typically unreleased: you close the mouth at the relevant place of articulation without adding an audible “puh”, “tuh”, or “kuh”.

- `sap6`: end with your lips closed.
- `sat6`: end with the tongue in position.
- `sik6`: end with the back of your tongue raised.

### Which Finals Are Possible?

Jyutping has a set of established vowel-plus-ending combinations. You cannot freely combine every vowel with every consonant.

For example, `-p`, `-t`, and `-k` occur with specific vowels, and not every combination exists in standard Cantonese. The official chart is the best reference for the complete inventory.

## 6. The Six Cantonese Tones

Cantonese has **six contrastive tone categories** in standard Jyutping. The tone number appears at the end of each syllable.

The easiest way to learn them is to practise the same syllable with all six tones.

| Tone | Pitch pattern | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | High level | `si1` 詩 | poem |
| 2 | High rising | `si2` 史 | history |
| 3 | Mid level | `si3` 試 | try |
| 4 | Low falling | `si4` 時 | time |
| 5 | Low rising | `si5` 市 | market/city |
| 6 | Low level | `si6` 事 | matter/thing |

Pitch is relative: these are approximate shapes, not fixed musical notes.

### Rules for Tones

**Rule 16: The number belongs to the syllable before it.**

For example, `nei5 hou2` means “hello” (literally, “you good”). The `5` belongs to `nei`; the `2` belongs to `hou`.

**Rule 17: Tone is not the same as loudness.**

Don't shout tone 1 or whisper tone 6. The main difference is pitch, not volume.

**Rule 18: Tones 1, 3 and 6 are level tones.**

They generally stay steady rather than rising or falling. Their pitch heights differ:

- Tone 1: high
- Tone 3: middle
- Tone 6: low

**Rule 19: Tones 2 and 5 rise.**

Tone 2 rises from a higher starting pitch than tone 5. Tone 5 starts low and rises.

**Rule 20: Tone 4 falls.**

Tone 4 is low and falling, although the exact pitch shape varies between speakers.

**Rule 21: Tone numbers are essential.**

Changing a tone can change a word's meaning, even when the initials and finals are identical. When learning vocabulary, memorise the complete syllable, including its tone.

### What About the “Nine Tones”?

You may hear that Cantonese has nine tones. This is a traditional way of counting them: the six main tone categories plus three additional categories associated with syllables ending in `-p`, `-t`, or `-k`.

Those short, stopped syllables are called *checked syllables*. In the standard Jyutping system, they still use tone numbers 1, 3, or 6, rather than having separate tone numbers 7, 8, and 9.

## 7. Syllables Without an Initial

Not every syllable starts with a consonant.

**Rule 22: If a syllable starts with a vowel sound, Jyutping does not add a special initial letter.**

Examples:

- `aa3` — a syllable used in words or particles such as 呀, depending on context
- `oi3` — love
- `aai3` — to sigh
- `ou3` — to vomit

You pronounce the vowel directly, rather than adding a glottal consonant or an English-style H.

## 8. The Two Standalone Nasal Syllables

Jyutping also has two syllables that consist of a nasal sound without an ordinary vowel.

| Jyutping | Pronunciation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `m4` | A syllabic `m`, with lips closed | not |
| `ng5` | A syllabic `ng`, with the back of the tongue raised | five |

**Rule 23: These are complete syllables on their own.**

For `m4`, keep your lips closed and produce the nasal sound. For `ng5`, start with the nasal sound in *sing* and hold it without adding a vowel.

These are particularly useful words to learn because they occur frequently in everyday Cantonese.

## 9. Pronunciation Changes You Will Hear in Real Cantonese

The official Jyutping spelling represents the standard pronunciation, but everyday speech isn't always identical to the textbook version.

These are common variations, especially in Hong Kong Cantonese.

| Standard pronunciation | Variation you may hear | What changes? |
|---|---|---|
| `nei5` (you) | `lei5` | `n` becomes `l` |
| `ng5` (five) | `m5` | The initial nasal changes |
| `ngoi3` (love) | `oi3` | The initial `ng` is dropped |
| `gwok3` (country) | `gok3` | `gw` becomes `g` |
| Some `-ng` endings | `-n` endings | The final nasal changes |
| Some `-k` endings | `-t` endings | The final stop changes |

These are examples of pronunciation variation, not rules that you should apply to every word.

### Rule 24: Learn the Standard Form First

When reading Jyutping, use the spelling as your starting point. Then listen to native speakers to become familiar with local accents, fast speech and sound changes.

You don't need to imitate every variation to be understood.

## 10. How to Read Complete Jyutping Sentences

Once you can identify the initials, finals and tones, put them together.

### Example 1: 你好

**Jyutping:** `nei5 hou2`

- `nei5` — you, tone 5
- `hou2` — good, tone 2

**Meaning:** Hello! (Literally, “you good”.)

### Example 2: 我唔知

**Jyutping:** `ngo5 m4 zi1`

- `ngo5` — I/me
- `m4` — not
- `zi1` — know

**Meaning:** I don't know.

### Example 3: 你食咗飯未？

**Jyutping:** `nei5 sik6 zo2 faan6 mei6?`

**Meaning:** Have you eaten yet?

The question mark indicates the sentence's meaning; it doesn't add another Jyutping tone.

**Rule 25: Pronounce every syllable, but don't insert extra vowels.**

For example, `sik6 zo2` has two syllables. Don't turn it into “si-kuh zuh-oh”. Keep the final `k` in `sik6` short and unreleased, and move directly into `zo2`.

**Rule 26: Tone numbers are not pauses.**

In `nei5 hou2`, the `5` and `2` tell you the pitch of each syllable. They do not mean you should pause between the words.

**Rule 27: Natural speech connects syllables smoothly.**

You should learn to pronounce individual syllables correctly first, then practise joining them into phrases at a natural speed. Some tones may sound different in connected speech, but don't automatically change their numbers when reading standard Jyutping.
