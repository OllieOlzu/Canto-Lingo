# Cantonese Grammar Course --- General Rules

## The Rules English Doesn't Teach You

Cantonese often expresses ideas through word order, context, small
grammar words, and particles at the ends of sentences. You do not need
to translate every English word directly.

This file contains the **general grammar rules**: sentence structure,
omitted subjects, tense and aspect, classifiers, adjectives, and other
sentence patterns.

## Rule 1. Cantonese often follows English word order --- but not always

The basic sentence order is usually:

**Subject + Verb + Object**

  Cantonese               English
  ----------------------- ----------------------
  `ngo5 sik6 faan6`       I eat rice / a meal.
  `nei5 zung1ji3 gau2`    You like dogs.
  `keoi5 zung1ji3 ngo5`   They like me.

This is good news: you can often build a basic sentence in the same
order as English.

But there are important differences when you add time, location, or
other information.

## Rule 2. You can leave out the subject when everyone knows who you mean

English usually requires a subject: "I'm hungry", "Are you going?", "I
don't know".

Cantonese often doesn't.

The natural word for "hungry" is `ngo5 hou2 ngo6` (我好餓) --- "I'm very
hungry". The two similar-looking syllables have different tones and
meanings: `ngo5` means "I", while `ngo6` means "hungry".

Examples:

-   `ngo5 m4 zi1` --- I don't know.
-   `m4 zi1` --- Don't know.
-   `nei5 heoi3 bin1 dou6?` --- Where are you going?
-   `heoi3 sik6 faan6` --- Go eat / go have a meal.

The subject is omitted because the listener can infer it, or because the
speaker isn't specifying it.

## Rule 3. Cantonese doesn't use "am", "is", and "are" in the same way

English uses forms of "to be" constantly. Cantonese often doesn't need a
verb at all when describing a state.

-   `ngo5 hou2 ngo6` --- I'm hungry.
-   `nei5 hou2 leng3` --- You're very pretty.
-   `gam1 jat6 hou2 jit6` --- It's hot today.

But when you're identifying something as a noun, Cantonese uses `hai6`
(係), meaning "to be" or "to be the case".

-   `ngo5 hai6 hok6saang1` --- I'm a student.
-   `keoi5 hai6 ngo5 pang4jau5` --- They are my friend.
-   `ni1 go3 hai6 mat1je5?` --- What is this?

**Rule:** use `hai6` for identifying or equating things, but don't
automatically insert it before every English adjective.

## Rule 4. Questions often don't need "do", "does", or "did"

In English, you say "Do you like it?" In Cantonese, you can simply turn
the statement into a question using intonation or a question structure.

-   `nei5 zung1ji3 keoi5?` --- Do you like them?
-   `nei5 heoi3 bin1 dou6?` --- Where are you going?
-   `nei5 sik6 zo2 faan6 mei6?` --- Have you eaten yet?

Cantonese also has a very common yes/no question pattern:

**Verb + 唔 + same verb**

-   `heoi3 m4 heoi3?` --- Are you going or not?
-   `sik6 m4 sik6?` --- Do you eat it / can you eat it?
-   `hai6 m4 hai6?` --- Is it or isn't it?

This is called an affirmative-negative question. You don't need to add
an English-style "do".

## Rule 5. Time words often go before the verb

English can say "I ate yesterday" or "Yesterday, I ate". Cantonese
commonly places the time expression before the verb.

-   `ngo5 gam1 jat6 sik6 faan6` --- I eat / am eating a meal today.
-   `ngo5 cam4 jat6 heoi3 zo2 hok6haau6` --- I went to school yesterday.
-   `ngo5 ting1 jat6 heoi3` --- I'll go tomorrow.

The pattern is often:

**Subject + Time + Verb + Object**

Time words such as `gam1 jat6` (today), `cam4 jat6` (yesterday), and
`ting1 jat6` (tomorrow) help establish when the event occurs.

## Rule 6. Cantonese doesn't conjugate verbs for past, present, and future

English changes "eat" to "ate" or "will eat". Cantonese verbs generally
don't change form for tense.

Instead, Cantonese uses time words, context, and aspect markers.

-   `ngo5 sik6 faan6` --- I eat / I'm eating a meal.
-   `ngo5 sik6 zo2 faan6` --- I ate / have eaten a meal.
-   `ngo5 ting1 jat6 sik6 faan6` --- I'll eat a meal tomorrow.

Notice that `sik6` stays the same. The time expression or grammatical
marker tells you how to interpret the event.

The important aspect markers --- `zo2`, `gwo3`, and `gan2` --- are
covered in the other file.

## Rule 7. Every countable noun usually needs a classifier

English says "three dogs". Cantonese usually says the equivalent of
"three \[classifier\] dogs".

The pattern is:

**Number + classifier + noun**

  Cantonese            Meaning
  -------------------- ------------------------
  `jat1 go3 jan4`      one person
  `loeng5 zek3 gau2`   two dogs
  `saam1 bun2 syu1`    three books
  `jat1 bui1 seoi2`    one glass/cup of water

The classifier depends on the type of thing you're counting.

Common classifiers:

-   `go3` --- general classifier, used for many people and objects.
-   `zek3` --- often used for animals.
-   `bun2` --- books and other bound volumes.
-   `gaa3` --- vehicles and some other objects.
-   `bui1` --- cups or glasses of a drink.

**Rule:** don't automatically put the number directly before the noun.
Learn the classifier along with the noun.

## Rule 8. Adjectives can behave like verbs

In English, we say "I am happy". In Cantonese, an adjective can often
form the predicate without an equivalent of "am".

-   `ngo5 hou2 hoi1sam1` --- I'm happy.
-   `gam1 jat6 hou2 jit6` --- It's hot today.
-   `ni1 go3 hou2 gwai3` --- This is expensive.

Notice `hou2` here. It often means "very" or "quite" before an
adjective, but in some sentences it is also part of a natural
descriptive pattern.

**Rule:** don't assume `hou2` always means "good". In `hou2 jit6`, it
intensifies the adjective "hot".

## Rule 9. Question words usually stay in their normal sentence position

Cantonese commonly keeps a question word in the position where the
answer would go.

-   `nei5 heoi3 bin1 dou6?` --- Where are you going?
-   `nei5 zung1ji3 mat1 je5?` --- What do you like?
-   `keoi5 hai6 bin1 go3?` --- Who is it?

You generally don't move the question word to the beginning just because
English does.

## Rule 10. `ge3` connects descriptions to nouns

The particle 嘅 `ge3` is extremely common. One important function is to
mark possession or connect a description to a noun.

-   `ngo5 ge3 syu1` --- my book.
-   `nei5 ge3 gau2` --- your dog.
-   `hou2 leng3 ge3 faan6` --- a beautiful meal.
-   `ngo5 zung1ji3 ge3 jan4` --- the person I like.

There are more complex uses, but this is a good starting point.

**Rule:** `ge3` often connects information before it to the noun that
follows. It is not always translated into a separate English word.

## Rule 11. `dou1` means "also", "all", or "even" depending on context

This is another word worth learning early.

-   `ngo5 dou1 zung1ji3` --- I also like it.
-   `keoi5 dei6 dou1 lai4 zo2` --- They all came.
-   `gam2 dou1 dak1?` --- Is even that okay? (depending on context)

It often appears after the subject and before the verb or adjective:

**Subject + `dou1` + predicate**

For example, `ngo5 dou1 sik6` means "I eat too" or "I also eat",
depending on the conversation.

## Rule 12. Don't translate particles one by one

Small words at the ends of sentences can change the tone, attitude, or
conversational meaning without changing the basic facts.

English also does this with intonation and expressions like "right?",
"you know", and "come on". Cantonese uses sentence-final particles very
frequently.

For example:

-   `hai6` --- it is / yes.
-   `hai6 aa3` --- yes, that's right.
-   `hai6 me1?` --- really? / is that so?
-   `hai6 lo1` --- exactly / that's how it is, depending on context.

The core statement is similar, but the particle changes the
conversational effect.

**The best way to learn particles is through complete sentences and
situations, not by memorising a single English translation.**

## Rule 13. Learn grammar through complete expressions and context

Don't try to memorise every particle in one sitting. Instead:

1.  Learn the foundations first.
2.  Study particles through real conversations and notice how they
    change a sentence's feeling.
3.  Practise one rule at a time: make five simple sentences using the
    rule, then listen to native Cantonese.
4.  Use Jyutping captions: pause at a phrase you don't understand,
    identify its grammar pattern, and translate the entire phrase rather
    than each word separately.

The most important principle is this: **learn what a Cantonese
expression does in a sentence, not just what it translates to in
English.** That's how the grammar starts to feel natural rather than
like a list of exceptions.
